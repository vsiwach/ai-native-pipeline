01

Agents are about to break every enterprise AI budget

Companies are moving from chatbots to agents — AI that completes whole tasks: writing and testing code, processing claims, resolving support tickets. Agents work in long loops and consume roughly 15× more compute per request than a chat query; agent traffic already exceeds human traffic on major platforms. For any company deploying agents seriously, inference — the cost of running AI — is becoming a top-three technology line item. And today most of that spend goes to a few frontier labs at premium prices, on one vendor’s chips, with the company’s data leaving its walls on every request.

02

What Modular does

THE RIGHT CHIP FOR EACH JOB

Serving AI has two phases: reading the input (compute-hungry) and writing the output (memory-hungry). Modular splits them — disaggregation — and runs each on the hardware that does it cheapest. Output generation, which dominates agent workloads, runs on memory-rich silicon (AMD today, Qualcomm AI200/AI250 next) that independent analysts measure at up to 40% lower cost per token.

ONE SOFTWARE LAYER, EVERY CHIP

Modular’s compiler technology — from the team behind Swift and LLVM — runs the same model on NVIDIA, AMD, or Qualcomm silicon without a rewrite. Customers are never locked in, and when any chipmaker cuts prices, the platform captures the savings automatically. The advantage isn’t one chip; it’s being first to the economics of every chip.

THEIR DATA STAYS HOME

High-volume, repeatable tasks run on open-weight models the customer controls — deployed inside their own cloud, tuned on their own data — so their know-how never leaves or improves someone else’s model. The hardest reasoning still reaches frontier models through a controlled gate. Frontier quality where needed; 10× cheaper everywhere else.

IN ONE SENTENCE

Modular runs a company’s AI tasks at the lowest proven cost per task, on whatever silicon is cheapest that month, with the company’s data and know-how staying inside its own walls.

03

Why this business survives

The market has already voted that cheap open-model inference is a real business: Fireworks (~$800M annualized revenue), Together (~$1B), and Baseten ($5B valuation) grow fast serving these workloads. But they all run the same engines on the same rented NVIDIA fleets — a price war between identical cost structures. Modular is the only player whose advantage comes from the hardware layer itself, and — with Qualcomm — the only one with preferential access to a new class of inference silicon in 2026–27. When margins compress industry-wide, the structural low-cost producer wins. That is our seat.

ALWAYS ON		 Developers, via self-serve endpoints. A funnel and credibility layer, not a revenue engine. Switching providers is trivially easy, so bottom-up usage alone never converts to enterprise commitments — fund it modestly, judge it as marketing.

The discipline: marquee accounts are the wedge, enterprise dedicated is the business, developer self-serve is marketing — in that order. Motion two does not scale spend until motion one has produced public, independently verifiable numbers.

06

The risks, honestly

NVIDIA cuts prices or gives software away. It already does the latter. We don’t sell a static discount — we sell the machine that always finds the cheapest silicon, whoever makes it. Chip price wars help us.

Marquee customers squeeze margins and can leave. True. We contract committed volumes, keep the roster small, and treat them as lighthouses funding the enterprise phase — not the end state.

Neutrality doubts under Qualcomm. Customers must believe we route to the best chip, not the parent’s. Neutrality is contractually secured in the acquisition agreement — independent benchmarks, published routing records, audit rights — and made visible every release, so it is observed, not claimed.

Discipline on new silicon. The plan assumes nothing from Qualcomm chips before 2028. AI200/AI250 and the enterprise channel are upside, not load-bearing — the 2026–27 business stands on hardware that exists today.
