01

Why we exist

Enterprises are moving from chatbots to agents — AI that completes whole tasks: writing and testing code, processing claims, resolving tickets, researching accounts. Agents work in long loops, consuming roughly 15× more compute per request than chat; agent traffic already exceeds human-initiated traffic on major platforms. For any company deploying agents seriously, inference is becoming a top-three technology line item — paid mostly to a few frontier labs at premium prices, on one vendor’s chips, with the company’s data leaving its walls on every request.

Those companies will run their tasks with their own context on open-weight and tuned models if — and only if — someone makes it decisively cheaper at proven quality. The market has confirmed this is a real business: Fireworks (~$800M annualized revenue), Together (~$1B ARR), and Baseten ($5B valuation) are growing fast serving exactly these workloads. But all of them rent the same NVIDIA fleets and run the same open-source engines, so their cost structures are identical and their margins converge to the price war.

Modular is the only endpoint provider with a structural, regenerating cost advantage: the compiler layer beneath everyone else’s stack. That is our reason for existence. Everything else in this document is machinery.

02

The game: market, physics, and the winner’s profile

2.1 · The endpoint market is proven and compounding

Endpoints — APIs serving open-weight and customer-tuned models, from shared serverless to dedicated capacity to deployments inside the customer’s own cloud — are how this market buys. Demand today is led by AI-native companies (coding agents, voice, avatars, healthcare AI) for whom tokens are cost-of-goods-sold and a 20–40% cost delta is existential. Behind them, enterprise agent deployments arrive on a 12–24 month lag with the same cost pain plus one requirement the AI-natives don’t have: context, adapters, and evaluation data must stay inside their perimeter. Modular Cloud already has the right commercial shape — shared endpoints, dedicated endpoints, custom models, our-cloud or your-cloud — serving the latest open models (MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2, Kimi K2.7, DeepSeek, Gemma 4) with day-0 coverage on NVIDIA and AMD.

2.2 · The physics favor us: decode dominates, and decode is a memory problem

Serving AI has two phases: reading the input (compute-hungry) and generating the output (memory-hungry). Agentic and high-volume workloads are dominated by generation, which is bandwidth-bound. That makes memory-rich silicon structurally cheaper for the dominant phase — independently measured at up to 40% lower cost per million tokens (AMD MI355X vs NVIDIA B200) — and makes disaggregation (running each phase on independently scaled, independently chosen hardware pools) the highest-leverage serving architecture in the industry. It is why NVIDIA built Dynamo. The difference: Dynamo disaggregates across NVIDIA chips only. We disaggregate across vendors.

Endpoint serving commoditizes: OpenAI-compatible APIs make switching trivial, so price-per-quality is the whole game. When every competitor’s cost structure is “NVIDIA list price plus a free engine,” nobody wins the price war — they fund it. 2.4 · The memory wall: why every roadmap in the industry now agrees with us

The physics of §2.2 has a market beneath it. HBM — the fast memory decode depends on — is 35–47% of an AI chip’s build cost, draws roughly a quarter of its power, carries a 5–6× per-GB premium over commodity DRAM, and all three suppliers’ 2026 output sold out by Q1, driving the worst memory price shock in fifteen years. Meanwhile KV cache grows with every concurrent user and every token of context, so the scarcest, most expensive component is the binding one. The interconnect makes it worse: PCIe Gen5 moves ~2% of HBM bandwidth, so any context that transits the host CPU dies waiting — a 50GB KV state that moves in 15ms inside HBM takes ~800ms over PCIe. Every serious player is now answering the same question — how to serve more tokens with less HBM — and their answers converge on our thesis:

A memory-first, HBM-free inference stack — worthless without a compiler that can program a novel memory hierarchy. That compiler is why they bought us.

The strategic reading: the industry’s own roadmaps have converged on programming the memory hierarchy — HBM to LPDDR to DRAM to SSD, stitched by fabrics that bypass the CPU — as the decisive battleground. That is a control-plane-plus-compiler problem. It is our problem, and nobody else owns both layers.

03

What we learned, and how it shapes the strategy

Every load-bearing belief in this strategy was independently validated against published evidence. The findings, in plain language:

Intelligence is jagged. Model capability varies sharply by task, tracking training data and the reward functions of RL post-training, which concentrate gains on verifiable domains (math, code, agentic tool use). Consequence: quality bars must be set per task, not per model — which makes migrating a route to a cheaper model a certifiable engineering decision rather than a leap of faith. Certification is our conversion machinery.

Tuned open models win narrow, verifiable, high-volume tasks. Reinforcement fine-tuning against checkable rewards reliably converts a small model’s latent ability into deployed reliability: documented cases show 8–14B open models matching or beating frontier APIs on specific enterprise tasks at 10–64× lower cost. Scope is strict — narrow tasks, verifiable outcomes, behavior not knowledge — and we sell it only that way: “attested parity on your route at a fraction of the cost,” never “smarter than frontier.”

Sovereignty gates the deployment envelope, not the vendor. Enterprise anxiety about data leaving the perimeter is real and measurable, but enterprises still send 88% of API spend to three closed labs. We do not claim they will stop. We sell the envelope: in-perimeter deployment for what must stay home, and a policy firewall that decides — and proves — what may go out to frontier models, stripped of protected context.

Open weights do not win by default. Enterprise open-weight share actually declined in 2025. Open models win where someone makes them decisively cheaper at proven quality, or where policy forces the perimeter. Both are our product. We are the mechanism by which the share reverses for the segments we serve — we do not assume the market does it for us.

The frontier labs’ own pain validates the compiler bet. Even the best-funded labs run three separate teams porting models across GPU, TPU, and custom silicon — and it works badly. Hardware diversity is increasing, kernel engineers are the scarcest talent in the industry, and per-target teams do not scale. One portable stack is the only answer that compounds.

04

The agentic kernel supply chain: day-0 optimized on any silicon

Two settled facts anchor this section: the Mojo compiler ships open source (Apache 2.0) in fall 2026 — publicly committed by the CEO and reaffirmed after the acquisition announcement — and vendor neutrality is contractually secured in the acquisition agreement.

4.1 · Compute is scarce; software is the real bottleneck

In a compute-scarce market, buyers increasingly take whatever silicon they can procure. The binding constraint is time-to-economics on that silicon: every new chip ships quarters ahead of the optimized software that makes it worth running. Whoever converts new hardware into certified, optimized serving fastest captures the scarcity premium on every generation. That is a compiler-and-agents problem, and it is the problem we are structurally built for.

4.2 · The flywheel: open compiler → agents that write Mojo → harness as reward function

Open source is the training-data strategy. Agents get good at languages they see in the wild. Opening the Mojo compiler — with the stdlib and ~500K lines of kernels already public — makes every AI coding agent progressively better at writing Mojo. Deepening Python interoperability gives developers the full capability of the hardware without understanding compilers or kernels: stay in Python, drop into Mojo only where performance demands it.

The agent skills already work in production. Modular ships an open skills repo (import-model, debug-model, kernel translation) that plugs into AI coding tools. Proven results: CUDA kernels translated to Mojo running on NVIDIA, AMD, and Apple silicon; a 192-expert MoE brought up via agent-driven scaffolding with layer-by-layer numerical verification; a customer’s agent-ported kernel beating its hand-written original unoptimized.

Kernels are the perfect verifiable-reward task. Correctness is machine-checkable and speedup is measurable — exactly the regime where reinforcement learning demonstrably works. Our public benchmark harness doubles as the reward function: agents propose kernels, the harness verifies numerics and measures cost at SLO, and fine-tuning improves the kernel-writing policy over time. Our proof infrastructure and our kernel supply chain become the same asset.

The payoff compounds into the moat. “First to the economics of every chip” today costs compiler-engineer headcount; with the agentic pipeline it compresses from quarters to weeks and scales without hiring.

4.3 · The hardest question: can a language beat NVIDIA at its own game?

The sharpest threat is not competitors’ kernels — it is silicon-specific model artifacts. Optimizers now release checkpoints quantized in NVIDIA-native formats (NVFP4), validated on named NVIDIA SKUs. If the canonical release of every open model ships pre-married to NVIDIA, every other chip starts a step behind before our compiler ever sees the weights. Three answers:

Formats are a translation problem, and the compiler is the best-placed layer to absorb them. MAX already serves FP8/FP4; open format standards (MXFP4) exist. We ingest NVIDIA-native checkpoints and run them at parity on NVIDIA, and requantize to open formats for other silicon with published accuracy deltas. Hand-tuners cannot do the reverse.

Specialists win cells; we win the matrix. Hand-tuning one model for one SKU is the “three porting teams” problem at ecosystem scale. The matrix — models × SKUs × formats — grows faster than any specialist roster. But revenue concentrates in the head of that matrix, so we must also match hand-tuned artifacts on NVIDIA’s own silicon for the top models, publicly, per release. Win the head at parity, and the tail plus every new chip is an unanswerable compound. Lose the head, and the matrix is a consolation prize. This is why the public harness gates everything.

The upstream counter: model-lab release partnerships. The durable answer to artifact lock-in is to make the canonical checkpoint multi-silicon from birth. We offer the model labs (Zhipu, Moonshot, MiniMax, DeepSeek, Google/Gemma) our day-0 pipeline and agent skills as a release service: launch with us and your model ships optimized on NVIDIA, AMD, and Qualcomm simultaneously, in open formats, with parity reports. Every lab that releases this way makes NVIDIA-native packaging one option among several instead of the default.

The same logic disposes of the newest specialization extreme. Transformer-only ASICs (Etched’s Sohu: ~$1B raised, $1B+ in claimed contracts, first racks promised summer 2026) are the maximum-lock-in bet — the model architecture burned into silicon. Their weakness is the matrix’s growth: the original design predated MoE dominance and had to be reworked, silicon cycles run 18–24 months while model architectures shift every six, and decode remains bound by the same HBM everyone buys. Their claims today are entirely company-published — pre-Phase-0 by our own standards. If they ship and survive independent benchmarks, heterogeneity increases and the buyer’s need for certified, reversible placement across pool types increases with it: an ASIC pool for stable head routes, programmable silicon for everything else, our harness and ledger arbitrating. Either way, the plane wins.

4.4 · The enterprise product: day-0 hardware adoption as a service

For enterprises and neoclouds with owned or committed fleets, the offer is simple: whatever hardware you can get, we make it serve optimally — in days. New chip lands → the agent pipeline ports and optimizes their models, adapters, and tuned checkpoints → the harness certifies quality and cost → endpoints go live inside their perimeter. Compute scarcity converts from their problem into our sales motion, and every silicon generation becomes a renewal event instead of a migration crisis.

Honest bounds: today’s proven claim is that agents compress model bring-up and porting — demonstrated on real MoE bring-ups — not that agents write our fastest kernels; expert production kernels still win, and the agent-written-kernel claim is a 2027 bet gated on hardened, anti-gaming verification. The industry’s loudest failure in this space was a gamed correctness check that inflated claimed speedups by 10×; our proof-first brand cannot afford that mistake, so no public agentic-kernel claim ships before the harness is adversarially hardened.

4.5 · The factory and the field: how agent-written kernels actually ship

Software-defined networking solved this exact deployment problem twenty years ago: programs authored and validated centrally, compiled artifacts pushed to devices, a control plane deciding what runs where. Mojo plays the role of P4 — the portable language that programs silicon the operator doesn’t fully control — with one advantage P4 never had: a hardware champion (Qualcomm) that doesn’t demand exclusivity. We apply the same split. Most of the win isn’t agents at all — it is the compiler, deterministically: fed the customer’s observed workload shapes from the ledger, MAX recompiles and autotunes generic serving into workload-specialized serving, in-perimeter, with no code generation and no access to customer code. Agents do the expensive creative work in the factory — new architectures, new devices, new fusion patterns — made smarter by the open Mojo corpus and community contributions, verified by the harness, human-gated, and shipped as signed artifacts. The field selects, composes, and specializes from that verified library against live traffic. Genuinely novel needs route back to the factory — or, for sovereign customers, through an in-perimeter replica of the harness with identical gates.

4.6 · Cross-silicon disaggregation: the three-rung ladder

The fair objection to cross-vendor serving is that it seems to need industry adoption Modular cannot force. It doesn’t — if climbed in order:

Rung 1 — route-level placement (now, nobody’s permission needed). Whole routes land on whole pools: this route on AMD, that one on NVIDIA. No KV state ever crosses a vendor boundary; the “interconnect” is a load balancer. This is most of the economic win, and it ships in 2026 regardless of what any chipmaker does.

Rung 2 — request-level disaggregation inside our own fleet (2027, engineering-gated, not adoption-gated). NVIDIA prefill feeding AMD/Qualcomm decode. The common intuition that this needs a shared memory fabric is wrong: KV transfer over standard RDMA Ethernet runs in production at extreme scale today (Moonshot’s Mooncake streams KV layer-by-layer across thousands of nodes). What actually gates it is KV layout control on both ends — which a compiler that owns the kernels on both sides uniquely has — and cross-vendor FP8/FP4 numerics, which is why the PRD gates this honestly. Done inside our fleet, where we control both ends; no vendor can practically block it.

Rung 3 — the open standard (the coalition play). For this to become an ecosystem rather than our party trick: publish an open KV interchange format — layouts, quantization scaling recipes, transfer semantics; the ONNX of inference state — with Qualcomm and AMD as founding implementers, Apple as the unified-memory proof point and quiet UALink ally, and NVIDIA arriving last, dragged by its own customers the same way hyperscaler pressure opened NVLink into Fusion. This is the LLVM playbook run a third time: author the neutral substrate everyone standardizes on.

The open-source strategy: how trust and compatibility move the cloud business

An inference platform asks a customer to do something unreasonable: put production traffic, model weights, and perimeter data on a young company. Every layer of closedness makes that ask harder. Open source is not community goodwill here — it is the answer to the four objections that actually stall deals: lock-in (“if Modular disappoints, we self-host the same Apache stack — leaving is a config change, not a rewrite”), auditability (“the kernels serving our traffic are inspectable source, not closed vendor blobs”), verification (“their benchmark harness and their kernels rerun on our machines”), and longevity (“the code outlives any corporate decision”). The funnel that follows is the proven one — developer adopts free and self-hosted, trust accumulates through use, the workload graduates to Modular Cloud for capacity, operations, and day-0 SLAs, and converts to dedicated or BYOC. Spark built Databricks this way.

5.2 · What the Mojo team owes the cloud business

Stability over brilliance. After 1.0, the language surface freezes and stays boring: great error messages, great docs, no feature churn taxing the kernel contributors, the MAX team, and the agent factory that build on it. Every roadmap item carries a tag: which endpoint metric does this move?

Python interop is the funnel’s mouth. Nobody adopts a new language to serve a model; they adopt a Python API that quietly got 30% cheaper. The team’s business KPI is not Mojo adoption — it is “the Python developer never left Python, and their endpoint cost dropped anyway.”

Agents are the primary developers now. The skills repo means coding agents write more new Mojo than humans will. Compiler diagnostics are reward signals; documentation is agent context. Designing for this is the strongest form of the typed-feedback bet against the Python-DSL ecosystem — and no other language team is positioned to make it.

5.3 · What the MAX team owes the cloud business

The drumbeat is the marketing. The six-week release cadence — new architectures, quantization coverage, speculative decoders, OpenAI-compatibility hardening — is the visible pulse that tells the market the stack is alive. The unglamorous work converts best: compatibility flags that make real-world requests just work do more for funnel conversion than a hero benchmark, because switching cost, not peak speed, is what keeps a customer on their incumbent.

The strip-mining objection answers itself. Once MAX is excellent, a hyperscaler can serve it — the classic attack on OSS companies. But this strategy already holds that the engine is not the moat; the ledger, the fleet-wide shape library, the agent factory, the hardware channel, and the day-0 SLA are. Anyone arguing against openness on competitive grounds is really arguing we don’t believe our own moat thesis — the cleanest internal test of strategic honesty we have.

The community is an extension of the factory. External kernel contributions run through the same harness as factory agents’ — numerics, anti-gaming, $/Mtok — and ship with the same signed provenance enterprises require. One verification pipeline, three sources of supply: staff, agents, community. That turns “contributions per quarter” from a vanity metric into kernel supply-chain growth.

5.4 · The scoreboard that ties both teams to the cloud

Time-to-first-endpoint for an open-source user (minutes, not days); OSS-to-cloud conversion per quarter; share of cloud incidents traced to engine regressions — MAX reliability is cloud reliability; day-0 lag on top-5 model families; external benchmark reruns within ±10%; agent success rate writing Mojo vs Python DSLs, published; community kernels passing harness verification. Move those seven numbers and the cloud becomes the inference platform of choice as a consequence: trust in, workloads follow, operations monetize.

What we explicitly do not believe

That the router is the business. Routing is commoditized from above and below. Routing and certification are how we win routes onto our endpoints — the endpoint serve is the business.

That a static cost advantage is a moat. NVIDIA has 75 points of gross margin to compress any spread. The moat is the machine that regenerates advantage: compiler portability, cross-vendor disaggregation, agentic bring-up, and first-to-economics on each new chip.

That token resale is a business. Customer-owned keys, zero markup, keys in customer KMS. We monetize serving and proof, never other people’s tokens.

That developer self-serve converts to enterprise revenue by itself. Commits come from capacity pricing, the perimeter, and accumulated tuned assets. Self-serve is funded as marketing.

That open weights win by default. They win where we make them decisively cheaper at certified quality, or where policy forces the perimeter. We build the mechanism; we do not assume the market.

That fine-tuned models out-reason frontier models. Tuning narrow verifiable routes is economics with a certification harness — never an intelligence claim.
